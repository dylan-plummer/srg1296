\begin{abstract}
We construct strongly regular graphs with the Latin square parameters $\LS_m(36)=(1296,35m,\,34+(m-1)(m-2),\,m(m-1))$ for $m=11,\dots,16$, and with the negative Latin square parameters $\NL_{16}(36)=(1296,592,268,272)$, all of which were open. With orthogonal arrays and known constructions, a strongly regular graph with parameters $\LS_m(36)$ now exists for every $m$. Our graphs are Cayley graphs of $\Z_2^4\times\Z_3^4$, the additive group of $\F_{16}\times\F_{81}$, and none of them is the graph of an orthogonal array. They were found by a constraint-programming search over groups of field multiplications and by joining complete catalogs of the possible cosets of $\F_{81}$ in the connection set.

The connection sets share a rigid structure. For $m\le18$ the part in $\F_{16}\times\{0\}$ and the dual part form planes, quadrics or their complements in $\mathrm{PG}(3,2)$, depending only on $m\bmod 8$; every other coset of $\F_{81}$ is one of nine local types; and symmetries of orders five and three organize the cases $m=13,\dots,16$ and $m=11,12$. Spreads link the parameter sets. An $\LS_{m+1}(n)$ graph contains a spanning $\LS_m(n)$ graph exactly when it is obtained from it by adding the cliques on a partition into $n$-cocliques. For our graphs this gives exactly $25$ graphs with parameters $\LS_{17}(36)$, among them a vertex-transitive graph that is not a Cayley graph and one that is a Cayley graph only of non-abelian groups. Some of our $\LS_{16}(36)$ graphs arise from a symmetric Bush-type Hadamard matrix by deleting three orthogonal spreads, which answers a question of Kova\v{l}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A strongly regular graph $\srg(v,k,\lambda,\mu)$ is a $k$-regular graph on $v$ vertices, neither complete nor edgeless, in which any two adjacent vertices have exactly $\lambda$ common neighbors and any two distinct non-adjacent vertices exactly $\mu$. Brouwer maintains an online table of feasible parameter sets~\cite{BrouwerTable}; see also the monograph of Brouwer and Van Maldeghem~\cite{BvM2022}.

The Latin square parameters $\LS_m(n)$ (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}) are those of the graph of an orthogonal array $\mathrm{OA}(n,m)$, equivalently of $m-2$ mutually orthogonal Latin squares of order $n$. For $n=36$ only eight MOLS are known, so orthogonal arrays give $\LS_m(36)$ graphs only for $m\le10$ and, by complementation, $m\ge27$. When this work began, Brouwer's table also listed graphs for $m=18$ and $m=19$, and the parameter sets $\LS_m(36)$ with $11\le m\le17$, together with their complements, were open. We close all of them.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:complete}
For every $m$ with $1\le m\le35$ there is a strongly regular graph with parameters $\LS_m(36)=(1296,35m,\,34+(m-1)(m-2),\,m(m-1))$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
There are at least $2$, $3$, $1$, $2$, $2$, $14$ and $7$ pairwise non-isomorphic Cayley graphs of $\Z_2^4\times\Z_3^4$ that are strongly regular with the parameters $\LS_m(36)$, $m=11,\dots,16$, and $\NL_{16}(36)$,
\[
\begin{gathered}
(1296,385,124,110),\quad (1296,420,144,132),\quad (1296,455,166,156),\quad (1296,490,190,182),\\
(1296,525,216,210),\quad (1296,560,244,240),\quad (1296,592,268,272).
\end{gathered}
\]
None of those with Latin square parameters is the graph of an orthogonal array.
\end{theorem}

The parameters $\LS_{17}(36)=(1296,595,274,272)$ were first realized by Kova\v{l}~\cite{KovalMultiOrbit}, with six graphs that are not vertex-transitive. The Cayley graphs III.1--III.4 (Table~\ref{tab:classes}) were the first vertex-transitive examples; Kova\v{l} later found two more in the same models (Remark~\ref{rem:koval595}).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:595}
There are at least $11$ pairwise non-isomorphic vertex-transitive strongly regular graphs with parameters $(1296,595,274,272)$: nine Cayley graphs of $\Z_2^4\times\Z_3^4$, one graph that is a Cayley graph only of non-abelian groups, and one graph that is not a Cayley graph. Exactly $25$ isomorphism classes of graphs with these parameters contain one of our $\LS_{16}(36)$ graphs as a spanning subgraph.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, the orthogonal arrays ($m\le10$), Theorem~\ref{thm:595} ($m=17$), Lemma~\ref{lem:free} applied to graph I.6 with two or three of its free subgroups ($m=18,19$; Proposition~\ref{prop:spreads}), and complementation ($m\ge20$). For $m=36$ the parameters are those of the complete multipartite graph $K_{36\times36}$, which we exclude as trivial. A switching of binary fibers also gives many graphs with parameters $\LS_{11}$, $\LS_{12}$, $\LS_{16}(36)$ and $\NL_{16}(36)$ that are not vertex-transitive (Section~\ref{sec:switching}).

If $D$ is a $(v,k,\lambda,\mu)$ partial difference set (PDS) in a group $G$ with $D=D^{(-1)}$ and $1\notin D$, then the Cayley graph $\Cay(G,D)$ is an $\srg(v,k,\lambda,\mu)$~\cite[Proposition~1.1]{Ma1994}. Brady~\cite{Brady2025} turned this into a systematic computational program aimed at new strongly regular graphs, after constructing negative Latin square type PDSs in non-abelian groups of order~$64$~\cite{Brady2022}. Kova\v{l}~\cite[Section~5]{KovalNew} realized $\LS_8(18)$ and two negative Latin square parameter sets on $324$ vertices by PDSs in $\F_4\times\F_{81}$, using cyclotomic classes of $\F_{81}$~\cite[Construction~5.4]{KovalNew}. We replace $\F_4$ by $\F_{16}$.

Our connection sets decompose along the cosets $\{a\}\times\F_{81}$, the \emph{rings}, and divisibility of character sums constrains each ring set locally (Lemma~\ref{lem:div}). This decomposition organizes the paper. It forces the binary data of every $\LS_m(36)$ PDS in this group with $m\le18$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:fibredata}); it allowed complete catalogs of ring sets to be joined, which found the graphs with $m=11,\dots,15$ (Section~\ref{sec:found}); and it shows that the ring sets of all our graphs are of nine local types (Section~\ref{sec:alphabet}). Under a symmetry $\rho$ of order five, $m$ must be $5$, $6$, $13$, $14$, $15$ or $16$, and the zero ring is then a cyclotomic negative Latin square graph (Theorem~\ref{thm:familyexact}).

\emph{Outline.} Section~\ref{sec:prelim} collects the character lemmas and the forced binary data. Section~\ref{sec:graphs} gives the graphs and how they were found, Section~\ref{sec:shared} their common structure, and Section~\ref{sec:spreads} spreads, completions and the graphs with parameters $\LS_{17}(36)$. Sections~\ref{sec:known}--\ref{sec:verification} compare with known constructions and list open questions and the verification.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Latin square type parameters}
The \emph{Latin square} and \emph{negative Latin square} parameters~\cite[Section~2]{Ma1994} are, for $n\ge2$ and $m\ge1$,
\begin{align*}
\LS_m(n)&=\bigl(n^2,\,m(n-1),\,n-2+(m-1)(m-2),\,m(m-1)\bigr),\\
\NL_m(n)&=\bigl(n^2,\,m(n+1),\,-n+m^2+3m,\,m(m+1)\bigr).
\end{align*}
For $m\le n$ and $m\le n-2$ respectively these are the parameters of graphs that are neither complete nor edgeless. An $\LS_m(n)$ graph has eigenvalues $n-m$ and $-m$; an $\NL_m(n)$ graph has eigenvalues $m$ and $m-n$. The graph of an orthogonal array $\mathrm{OA}(n,m)$ (equivalently, of $m-2$ mutually orthogonal Latin squares of order $n$) is an $\LS_m(n)$ graph, and every edge of it lies in a clique of size $n$. For $n=36$ only eight MOLS are known, so known orthogonal arrays give $\LS_m(36)$ graphs only for $m\le10$ (and their complements); Brouwer's table~\cite{BrouwerTable} lists $\mathrm{OA}(36,10)$ as known and $\mathrm{OA}(36,11)$ as open.

\subsection{Partial difference sets and characters}
Let $G$ be a finite group of order $v$. A $k$-subset $D\subseteq G$ is a $(v,k,\lambda,\mu)$-\emph{partial difference set} (PDS) if the products $gh^{-1}$ with $g,h\in D$, $g\ne h$, represent every non-identity element of $D$ exactly $\lambda$ times and every non-identity element outside $D$ exactly $\mu$ times; it is \emph{regular} if $1\notin D$ and $D=D^{(-1)}$~\cite[Section~1]{Ma1994}. From now on $G$ is abelian and written additively, so a regular PDS satisfies $0\notin D=-D$. Let $(v,k,\lambda,\mu)$ be feasible with integral eigenvalues $r>s$, the roots of $x^2-(\lambda-\mu)x-(k-\mu)$, and let $D\subseteq G\setminus\{0\}$ with $D=-D$ and $|D|=k$.

\begin{lemma}[{\cite[Corollary~3.3]{Ma1994}}]\label{lem:char}
$D$ is a $(v,k,\lambda,\mu)$-PDS if and only if $\chi(D)=\sum_{d\in D}\chi(d)\in\{r,s\}$ for every non-principal character $\chi$ of $G$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[{\cite[Theorem~4.1]{Ma1994}}]\label{lem:mult}
Let $D$ be a regular PDS in an abelian group $G$. If $(\lambda-\mu)^2+4(k-\mu)$ is a perfect square, then $tD=D$ for every integer $t$ coprime to $|G|$.
\end{lemma}
In particular, a PDS with integral eigenvalues is a union of orbits of the multiplier group $\{x\mapsto tx:\gcd(t,|G|)=1\}$. Any additional group of automorphisms that we impose on $D$ can therefore be taken to contain the multipliers, with no loss of generality.

\subsection{Fibers over $\F_{16}$}\label{sec:fibrelemmas}
From now on $G=\F_{16}\times\F_{81}$ (additive group), with $\F_{16}$ and $\F_{81}$ as in Section~\ref{sec:graphs}. Its characters are the products $\varphi\times\psi$ of characters $\varphi$ of $\F_{16}$ and $\psi$ of $\F_{81}$. Let $\mathrm{Tr}$ denote the absolute traces of $\F_{16}$ and $\F_{81}$, and $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. We identify $G$ with its character group by $(c,d)\mapsto\chi_{c,d}$,
\[
\chi_{c,d}(a,b)=(-1)^{\mathrm{Tr}(ca)}\,\omega^{\mathrm{Tr}(db)},
\]
and write $\varphi_c=\chi_{c,0}|_{\F_{16}}$ and $\psi_d=\chi_{0,d}|_{\F_{81}}$. For $D\subseteq G$, $a\in\F_{16}$ and $b\in\F_{81}$ let
\[
T_a=\{b':(a,b')\in D\}\subseteq\F_{81},\qquad U_b=\{a':(a',b)\in D\}\subseteq\F_{16}.
\]
We call the cosets $\{a\}\times\F_{81}$ \emph{rings} and $|T_a|$ the \emph{ring sizes}. Let $D$ be a regular PDS in $G$ with integral eigenvalues $r>s$, and put $\Delta=r-s$. For $\psi\ne1$ let $S_\psi=\{\varphi:(\varphi\times\psi)(D)=r\}$, for $\varphi\ne1$ let $S'_\varphi=\{\psi:(\varphi\times\psi)(D)=r\}$, and let $S_1=\{\varphi\ne1:(\varphi\times1)(D)=r\}$. Through $c\mapsto\varphi_c$ we regard $S_1$ as a subset of $\F_{16}^*$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:div}
For $\psi\ne1$ and $a\in\F_{16}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fibre}
\psi(T_a)=s\,[a=0]+\frac{\Delta}{16}\sum_{\varphi\in S_\psi}\varphi(a),
\end{equation}
and for $\varphi\ne1$ and $b\in\F_{81}$, $\varphi(U_b)=s\,[b=0]+\frac{\Delta}{81}\sum_{\psi\in S'_\varphi}\psi(b)$. If $\Delta=36$, then $\psi(T_a)\equiv s\,[a=0]\pmod 9$ and $\varphi(U_b)\equiv s\,[b=0]\pmod 4$ for all such $\varphi,\psi,a,b$, and $|S_\psi|$ is divisible by $4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\psi\ne1$. Every $\varphi\times\psi$ is non-principal, so $(\varphi\times\psi)(D)=s+\Delta\,[\varphi\in S_\psi]$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:char}. Since $(\varphi\times\psi)(D)=\sum_a\varphi(a)\psi(T_a)$, Fourier inversion on $\F_{16}$ gives~\eqref{eq:fibre}; the formula for $\varphi(U_b)$ is proved in the same way. Let $\Delta=36$. Since $-a=a$ in $\F_{16}$ and $D=-D$, we have $T_a=-T_a$, so $\psi(T_a)$ is a rational integer, and so is $W=\sum_{\varphi\in S_\psi}\varphi(a)$. By~\eqref{eq:fibre}, $\psi(T_a)-s[a=0]=9W/4$, so $4\mid W$ and $9\mid\psi(T_a)-s[a=0]$; for $a=0$ we have $W=|S_\psi|$. Similarly $\varphi(U_b)$ is an integer, and $X=\sum_{\psi\in S'_\varphi}\psi(b)=\frac94(\varphi(U_b)-s[b=0])$ is a rational algebraic integer, hence an integer, so $4\mid\varphi(U_b)-s[b=0]$.
\end{proof}

For $a\ne0$ write $T_a=\pm P$ or $T_a=\{0\}\cup\pm P$, with $P$ a set of points of $\mathrm{PG}(3,3)$. Then $\psi(T_a)=[0\in T_a]+3|P\cap H_\psi|-|P|$, where $H_\psi$ is the plane $\ker\psi$. So Lemma~\ref{lem:div} says that all plane sections of $P$ have sizes in one residue class modulo $3$. This is the condition behind the catalogs of Section~\ref{sec:fibres}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:trace}
Put $d=|T_0|$, $d'=|U_0|$, $j=|S_1|$ and $j'=|\{\psi\ne1:(1\times\psi)(D)=r\}|$. Then
\[
16d=k+15s+\Delta j,\qquad 81d'=k+80s+\Delta j',
\]
and $|T_a|=\frac{1}{16}\bigl(k-s+\Delta\sum_{\varphi\in S_1}\varphi(a)\bigr)$ for $a\ne0$. In particular, for $\LS_m(36)$ we have $4d=5m+9j$, $9d'=4j'-5m$ and $|T_a|=\frac94\bigl(m+\sum_{\varphi\in S_1}\varphi(a)\bigr)$ for $a\ne0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Summing $(\varphi\times1)(D)=\sum_a\varphi(a)|T_a|$ over all $\varphi$ gives $16|T_0|=k+(15-j)s+jr$. Summing $(1\times\psi)(D)=\sum_b\psi(b)|U_b|$ over all $\psi$ gives the second identity. Fourier inversion of $\varphi\mapsto(\varphi\times1)(D)$ gives the third.
\end{proof}


\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dual}
Let $D$ be a regular PDS in $G$ with parameters $\LS_m(36)$ or $\NL_m(36)$, and let $\theta\in\{r,s\}$ be the eigenvalue of multiplicity $k$, that is, $\theta=r$ for $\LS_m(36)$ and $\theta=s$ for $\NL_m(36)$. Then
\[
D^*=\{(c,d)\ne(0,0):\chi_{c,d}(D)=\theta\}
\]
is a regular PDS with the same parameters. Moreover, for $g\ne0$ we have $\chi_g(D^*)=\theta$ if and only if $g\in D$; in particular $D^{**}=D$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The partition $\{0\},D,G\setminus(D\cup\{0\})$ is a translation association scheme. Its dual scheme lives on the character group and has the classes $\{\chi:\chi(D)=r\}$ and $\{\chi:\chi(D)=s\}$~\cite{Delsarte1973}, so $D^*$ is a PDS. Its parameters are those of $D$, because the Latin square and negative Latin square parameters are formally self-dual: the first and second eigenmatrices of the scheme coincide. The dual of the dual scheme is the original scheme, which gives the second statement since $D=-D$. Independently, every $D^*$ used in this paper was checked by counting all differences.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Dual parity]\label{lem:parity}
Let $D$ be a regular PDS in $G$ with parameters $\LS_m(36)$. For $S\subseteq\F_{16}^*$ let
\[
\Pi_m(S)=\Bigl\{a\ne0:\ \tfrac94\bigl(m+\textstyle\sum_{c\in S}(-1)^{\mathrm{Tr}(ca)}\bigr)\ \text{is odd}\Bigr\}.
\]
Then $U_0=\Pi_m(S_1)$ and $S_1=\Pi_m(U_0)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $T_a=-T_a$ and $|\F_{81}|$ is odd, $0\in T_a$ if and only if $|T_a|$ is odd. By Lemma~\ref{lem:trace}, $|T_a|=\frac94(m+\sum_{c\in S_1}\varphi_c(a))$ for $a\ne0$, so $U_0=\Pi_m(S_1)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:dual}, $D^*$ has the same parameters, and $S_1(D^*)=\{a\ne0:(a,0)\in D\}=U_0(D)$ and $U_0(D^*)=\{c\ne0:\chi_{c,0}(D)=r\}=S_1(D)$. Applying the first statement to $D^*$ gives $S_1=\Pi_m(U_0)$.
\end{proof}

This involution is very restrictive. Identify $\F_{16}^*$ with the points of $\mathrm{PG}(3,2)$. For a non-degenerate quadratic form $Q$ on $\F_{16}$ over $\F_2$ we call $\{x\ne0:Q(x)=0\}$ an \emph{ovoid} ($5$ points) if $Q$ is elliptic and a \emph{grid} ($9$ points) if $Q$ is hyperbolic. We call $\{x:Q(x)=1\}$ the \emph{complement of the ovoid} ($10$ points) or of the grid ($6$ points), respectively.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fibredata}
Let $D$ be a regular PDS in $G$ with parameters $\LS_m(36)$, $1\le m\le18$. Then $S_1$ and $U_0$ are sets of the type given in Table~\ref{tab:fibredata}, which depends only on $m\bmod 8$, and
\[
|T_0|=\tfrac14\bigl(5m+9|S_1|\bigr).
\]
The ring sizes $|T_a|$, $a\ne0$, are as in the table, the odd value being taken exactly for $a\in U_0$. Conversely, every set of the given type satisfies these necessary conditions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:trace}, $S_1$ must make all values $\frac94(m+\sum_{c\in S_1}\varphi_c(a))$ integers in $[0,81]$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:parity}, $U_0=\Pi_m(S_1)$ and $\Pi_m(U_0)=S_1$. Since $U_0=S_1(D^*)$ and $D^*$ has the same parameters (Proposition~\ref{prop:dual}), $U_0$ must satisfy the same integrality condition; this excludes, for $m=4$ and $m=12$, the set $S_1=\emptyset$, for which $U_0=\F_{16}^*$ and $|T_0|$ would be odd. A computer enumeration of all $2^{15}$ subsets $S\subseteq\F_{16}^*$ shows that, for each $m$, the sets $S$ satisfying these conditions are exactly the sets of the type in the table; for these, $\Pi_m(S)$ has the same type. The formula for $|T_0|$ is Lemma~\ref{lem:trace}.
\end{proof}

\section{The graphs}\label{sec:graphs}

\subsection{Constructions}
Throughout, $\F_{16}=\F_2[x]/(x^4+x^3+1)$ and $\F_{81}=\F_3[y]/(y^4+y^3+2)$, with primitive elements $\alpha=x$ and $\beta=y$. Vertices are pairs $(a,b)\in\F_{16}\times\F_{81}$, and $(a,b)\sim(a',b')$ if and only if $(a-a',b-b')\in D$.

\begin{construction}[Graph I]\label{con:I}
Let
\[
\begin{aligned}
S_I=\{&(0,0),(0,2),(0,6),(0,11),(0,13),(0,14),(0,17),(0,18),(0,19),\\
&(1,3),(1,6),(1,7),(1,8),(1,10),(1,11),(1,13),(1,15),(1,19),\\
&(2,1),(2,2),(2,5),(2,9),(2,10),(2,11),(2,13),(2,16),(2,17)\}\subseteq\Z_3\times\Z_{20},
\end{aligned}
\]
and
\begin{multline*}
D_I=\{(0,\beta^{j}):j\equiv0\pmod 4\}\\
\cup\ \bigl\{(\alpha^i,\beta^j):0\le i<15,\ 0\le j<80,\ \bigl(i\bmod 3,\ (j-4\lfloor i/3\rfloor)\bmod 20\bigr)\in S_I\bigr\}.
\end{multline*}
Then $|D_I|=20+27\cdot20=560$. $D_I$ is invariant under $(a,b)\mapsto(\alpha^3a,\beta^4b)$, which has order $20$ and whose tenth power is $-1$, and under $(a,b)\mapsto(a^2,b^{27})$.
\end{construction}

\begin{construction}[Graph II]\label{con:II}
Let $J_a\subseteq\Z_{20}$ for $a\in\F_{16}$ be given by Table~\ref{tab:J}, and
\[
D_{II}=\{(a,\beta^j):a\in\F_{16},\ 0\le j<80,\ j\bmod 20\in J_a\}.
\]
Then $|D_{II}|=4\,(13+15\cdot9)=592$. $D_{II}$ is invariant under multiplication by $\mu_4=\langle\beta^{20}\rangle$ on the second coordinate and under $(a,b)\mapsto(a^2,b^3)$. Each slice $D_a=\{b:(a,b)\in D_{II}\}$ is a union of the cyclotomic classes $\beta^j\mu_4$ of index $20$. This is the shape of Kova\v{l}'s Construction~5.4 in~\cite{KovalNew}, with $\F_4$ replaced by $\F_{16}$.
\end{construction}

\begin{construction}[Graph III]\label{con:III}
Let $J'_a\subseteq\Z_{20}$ for $a\in\F_{16}$ be given by Table~\ref{tab:J}, and
\[
D_{III}=\{(a,\beta^j):a\in\F_{16},\ 0\le j<80,\ j\bmod 20\in J'_a\}\ \cup\ \bigl(\F_4^*\times\{0\}\bigr),
\]
where $\F_4^*=\{1,\alpha^5,\alpha^{10}\}$. Then $|D_{III}|=4\,(7+3\cdot11+12\cdot9)+3=595$. $D_{III}$ is invariant under multiplication by $\mu_4$ on the second coordinate and under $(a,b)\mapsto(a^2,b^3)$. The three slices with $|J'_a|=11$ are those with $a\in\F_4^*$.
\end{construction}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:graphs}
$D_I,\dots,D_{VIII}$ are partial difference sets in $(\F_{16}\times\F_{81},+)\cong\Z_2^4\times\Z_3^4$ with parameters $(1296,k,\lambda,\mu)$ for $(k,\lambda,\mu)=(560,244,240)$, $(592,268,272)$, $(595,274,272)$, $(525,216,210)$, $(490,190,182)$, $(455,166,156)$, $(420,144,132)$ and $(385,124,110)$. Consequently the Cayley graphs $\Gamma_I,\dots,\Gamma_{VIII}$ of $\F_{16}\times\F_{81}$ with these connection sets are strongly regular, with spectra $560^1\,20^{560}\,(-16)^{735}$, $592^1\,16^{703}\,(-20)^{592}$, $595^1\,19^{595}\,(-17)^{700}$, $525^1\,21^{525}\,(-15)^{770}$, $490^1\,22^{490}\,(-14)^{805}$, $455^1\,23^{455}\,(-13)^{840}$, $420^1\,24^{420}\,(-12)^{875}$ and $385^1\,25^{385}\,(-11)^{910}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
All eight sets satisfy $D=-D$ and $0\notin D$. The identity $A^2=kI+\lambda A+\mu(J-I-A)$ was verified exhaustively, by counting the common neighbors of all $\binom{1296}{2}$ vertex pairs of $\Cay(G,D)$ (Section~\ref{sec:verification}). As a consistency check, all $1295$ non-principal character values were computed numerically. They take exactly the two values $r,s$ allowed by Lemma~\ref{lem:char}, with the multiplicities of the stated spectrum.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Unlike Kova\v{l}'s Construction~5.4, which is proved by hand in~\cite[Section~5.3]{KovalNew}, these proofs are computational. A proof in that style should be possible, since every character value of $D_I$, $D_{II}$ and $D_{III}$ is a sum of Gauss periods of index $20$ of $\F_{81}$ with signs $\pm1$ given by additive characters of $\F_{16}$.
For $D_{IV}$, $D_V$ and $D_{VI}$, Section~\ref{sec:family} explains the shape of the sets $T_0$ and $U_0$ and the sizes of the sets $T_a$ by hand, but the existence proof is computational.
\end{remark}

\subsection{How the graphs were found}\label{sec:found}\label{sec:search}\label{sec:fibres}
\emph{Field-multiplication models.} For a subgroup $S\le\F_{16}^*\times\F_{81}^*$ and an optional Galois map $\boldsymbol\sigma=(a,b)\mapsto(a^{2^u},b^{3^w})$, let $K(S,\boldsymbol\sigma)$ be the group generated by $S$, $\boldsymbol\sigma$ and the multipliers $x\mapsto tx$, $\gcd(t,6)=1$; the multipliers contribute only $-1=(1,\beta^{40})$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:mult} a PDS with integral eigenvalues is invariant under the multipliers, so the character sums $c_{ji}=\chi_j(O_i)$ of the orbits $O_i$ of $K$ are rational integers, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:char} the $K$-invariant PDSs are exactly the $0/1$ solutions $(x,b)$ of
\[
\textstyle\sum_i|O_i|\,x_i=k,\qquad \sum_i c_{ji}\,x_i=s+(r-s)\,b_j\quad\text{for every non-principal dual orbit }\Omega_j.
\]
We solved these systems with CP-SAT~\cite{CPSAT}. This is Kova\v{l}'s cyclotomic search on $\F_4\times\F_{81}$~\cite[Section~5.4]{KovalNew} with $\F_4$ replaced by $\F_{16}$; since $\F_{16}^*\times\F_{81}^*\cong C_{15}\times C_{80}$ is not cyclic, $S$ runs over all $80$ subgroups, and $\boldsymbol\sigma$ over $16$ maps. The $1251$ of these $1280$ models with at most $200$ orbits were tested against every open parameter set $(1296,k,\lambda,\mu)$ with $k\le648$ and integral eigenvalues; solutions exist for $\LS_{16}(36)$, $\NL_{16}(36)$ and $\LS_{17}(36)$ (graphs I, II, III). A model without solutions is not a proof of non-existence: the model $\langle\rho\rangle$, $\rho=(\alpha^{12},\beta^{16})$, gave no decision for $\LS_{13}$, $\LS_{14}$ and $\LS_{15}(36)$ within $120$ seconds, but it has solutions.

\emph{Ring joins.} Write $D=\bigcup_a\{a\}\times T_a$. In the model $\langle\rho\rangle$ the ring sets on an orbit $\{\alpha^{12t}a\}$ are $T_{\alpha^{12t}a}=\beta^{16t}T_a$, so $D$ is determined by $T_0$ and three \emph{seeds} $T_1,T_\alpha,T_{\alpha^2}$. Writing a seed as $\pm P$ or $\{0\}\cup\pm P$ for a set $P$ of points of $\mathrm{PG}(3,3)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:div} says that every plane meets $P$ in a number of points congruent to $|P|$ modulo $3$, equivalently $\sum_{x\in P}xx^\top\equiv0\pmod 3$. A meet-in-the-middle enumeration over these quadratic moments lists all such sets in seconds: $9{,}880$ with $9$ points, $365{,}080$ with $13$ points and $2{,}677{,}740$ with $18$ points, counts confirmed by an independent dynamic program. For each dual orbit of $\psi$ the vector $(\psi(T_a))_a$ must have its Walsh transform over $\F_{16}$ in $\{r,s\}$; this couples the seeds, and the catalogs are joined with bit-set indices on these Walsh patterns. With optional restrictions on the positions of the positive Walsh coefficients (not necessary conditions), the joins found $D_{IV}$, $D_V$ and $D_{VI}$ in a few seconds each. The same join in the model $\langle\sigma\rangle$ of Section~\ref{sec:outside}, which has five seeds, found $D_{VII}$ after $33$ search nodes and $D_{VIII}$ after about $10^3$, again with optional restrictions. All searches stopped at the first solution, so they say nothing about how many solutions exist.

\subsection{Graphs that are not vertex-transitive}\label{sec:switching}

A switching of the binary fibers of our Cayley graphs gives many strongly regular graphs with the same parameters that are not vertex-transitive. Let $H_{ab}=(-1)^{a\cdot b}$ be the Walsh matrix of $\F_2^4$, let $M\subseteq\F_2^4\setminus\{0\}$, and put $e_a=-1$ for $a\in M$ and $e_a=1$ otherwise. Then $Q=H\,\mathrm{diag}(e)\,H/16$ is a symmetric orthogonal involution that fixes the all-ones vector; for $M=\F_2^4\setminus\{0\}$ it is the Godsil--McKay matrix $\tfrac{2}{16}J-I$. Order the vertices of $\Cay(G,D)$ by the $81$ binary fibers $\F_{16}\times\{b\}$, choose a set $S$ of fibers, and let $U$ be block diagonal with $Q$ on the fibers in $S$ and $I$ elsewhere. Every block of the adjacency matrix $A$ is invariant under binary translations, so it commutes with $Q$, and $A'=UAU$ differs from $A$ only in the blocks between $S$ and its complement. If $A'$ is a $0/1$ matrix, which is a finite check on the transformed blocks, then it is strongly regular with the parameters of $A$, since $U$ is orthogonal and fixes $I$ and $J$.

Taking $S$ to be a hyperplane of $\F_{81}$ gives graphs with automorphism groups of orders $864$, $864$, $1728$ and $864$ for $k=385$, $420$, $560$ and $592$. For $k=560$ and $k=592$ there are choices of $M$ for which every subset $S$ is admissible, giving exactly $2^{80}$ labelled graphs each. Counts of $4$-cliques through a vertex certify at least $68$ pairwise non-isomorphic graphs that are not vertex-transitive: one each for $k=385$ and $k=420$, and $33$ each for $k=560$ and $k=592$. These graphs, the choices of $M$ and $S$, and independent verifiers are in the data repository.

\subsection{A local alphabet}\label{sec:alphabet}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:alphabet}
Let $T=-T\subseteq\F_{81}$ have all nontrivial character sums divisible by $9$. Then
\[
\sum_d c_d(T)=\frac{81\,[0\in T]-|T|}{18},\qquad \sum_d c_d(T)^2=\frac{|T|\,(81-|T|)}{162}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Sum $\psi(T)$ over all $\psi\ne1$, and apply Parseval's identity. Note that $\psi_d$ and $\psi_{-d}$ give the same value.
\end{proof}

For $|T|=18,27,36,45$ this gives $(\sum c,\sum c^2)=(-1,7)$, $(3,9)$, $(-2,10)$, $(2,10)$, which leaves few possible multisets $\{c_d\}$. Across all thirty-five graphs, only nine occur (Table~\ref{tab:alphabet}). Three of them are classical: the type $L_1$ is $\pm$ an affine plane, that is, a hyperplane of $\F_{81}$ minus a $2$-dimensional subspace; the type $L_6$ is $\F_{81}$ minus two hyperplanes; and the type $L_8$ is the union of two hyperplanes. The same types recur for different $m$: every ring of size $27$, in all graphs with $11\le m\le15$, is of type $L_3$ or $L_4$, and $L_7$ occurs for $m=14,15,16,17$ and in the graphs II.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rings}
In $\Gamma_I$ each ring induces the Brouwer--Haemers graph $\srg(81,20,1,6)$~\cite{BrouwerHaemers1992}, namely the cyclotomic graph $\Cay(\F_{81},\langle\beta^4\rangle)$, and every vertex has exactly $36$ neighbors in each of the other $15$ rings. In $\Gamma_{II}$ each ring induces a $52$-regular graph that is not strongly regular, and every vertex has exactly $36$ neighbors in each other ring. In $\Gamma_{III}$ each ring induces a $28$-regular graph that is not strongly regular. A vertex of $R_a$ has $45$ neighbors in each of the three rings $R_{a+c}$, $c\in\F_4^*$, and $36$ in each of the other twelve rings.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The subgraph induced on $R_a$ is $\Cay(\F_{81},D\cap(\{0\}\times\F_{81}))$, and $D_I\cap(\{0\}\times\F_{81})=\{0\}\times\langle\beta^4\rangle$. This cyclotomic graph is an $\srg(81,20,1,6)$, as a direct count shows, and an $\srg(81,20,1,6)$ is unique~\cite{BrouwerHaemers1992}, so it is the Brouwer--Haemers graph. The remaining counts are $|D\cap(\{a\}\times\F_{81})|$ for $a\neq0$, read off from the constructions. For $\Gamma_{II}$, adjacent pairs in a ring have $31$ or $37$ common neighbors inside the ring, and for $\Gamma_{III}$ they have $1$, $7$ or $13$.
\end{proof}

The quotient matrix of the rings is $cI+36(J-I)$ with $c=20$ for $\Gamma_I$ and $c=52$ for $\Gamma_{II}$, with eigenvalues $k$ and $c-36=\mp16$; for $\Gamma_{III}$ the subfield $\F_4$ singles out three rings. So strong regularity of $\Gamma$ does not require strongly regular rings (Figure~\ref{fig:rings}). All fourteen graphs I share the ring structure of $\Gamma_I$. For IV, V and VI the rings induce $\NL_3(9)$, $\NL_4(9)$ and $\NL_5(9)$, and for VII and VIII they induce $42$- and $34$-regular graphs that are not strongly regular. An interactive visualization of the graphs I--III is in the data repository.

\subsection{The order-five family}\label{sec:family}
Let $\rho=(\alpha^{12},\beta^{16})$, the multiplication by elements of order $5$ in both fields. Up to isomorphism, the model $\langle\rho\rangle$ contains the graphs I.1--I.6 and I.11 (each is invariant under $(\alpha^3,\beta^{16c})$ for some $c$, and the Galois maps $b\mapsto b^{3^w}$ permute these four groups transitively), the graphs IV--VI, and with $D$ also $D^*$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:family}
Let $D$ be a $\rho$-invariant regular PDS in $G$ with parameters $\LS_m(36)$, $1\le m\le18$, or $\NL_m(36)$ with $k\le648$. Then $D$ has Latin square type and $m\in\{5,6,7\}\cup\{13,14,15,16\}$. Put $g=18-m$ and $e=g-2$ if $m\ge13$, and $g=9-m$ and $e=g-1$ if $m\le7$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $T_0$ is the union of $g$ of the eight cyclotomic classes $\beta^i\langle\beta^8\rangle$, and $\Cay(\F_{81},T_0)$ is a strongly regular graph with parameters $\NL_g(9)=(81,10g,g^2+3g-9,g^2+g)$;
\item[(b)] $U_0$ is the union of $e$ of the three cosets of $\mu_5=\langle\alpha^3\rangle$, so $\Cay(\F_{16},U_0)$ is the empty graph, the Clebsch graph $\srg(16,5,0,2)$, its complement $\srg(16,10,6,6)$, or $K_{16}$, for $e=0,1,2,3$;
\item[(c)] for $a\ne0$, $|T_a|=36-9\,[a\in U_0]$ if $m\ge13$, and $|T_a|=18-9\,[a\in U_0]$ if $m\le7$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The group $\langle\rho,-1\rangle$ has order $10$. Its orbits on $\F_{16}^*$ are the three cosets of $\mu_5$, and its orbits on $\F_{81}^*$ are the eight classes $\beta^i\langle\beta^8\rangle$, because $\langle\beta^{16},\beta^{40}\rangle=\langle\beta^8\rangle$. So $T_0$ is a union of classes and $U_0$ a union of cosets, and $10\mid d$, $5\mid d'$. Since $\mathrm{Tr}(c\cdot\zeta a)=\mathrm{Tr}(\zeta c\cdot a)$, the dual action of $\rho$ on characters is again multiplication by $(\alpha^{12},\beta^{16})$. So $S_1$ is a union of cosets of $\mu_5$ and $5\mid j$. Also $\{\psi\ne1:(1\times\psi)(D)=r\}$ is closed under $\rho$ and under complex conjugation, so $10\mid j'$. With these divisibilities, the two identities of Lemma~\ref{lem:trace} hold only for $\LS_m(36)$ with $m\in\{5,\dots,8\}\cup\{13,\dots,16\}$ and for $\NL_{10}(36)$. This is a finite check over $m$, recorded in the data repository. For $\LS_m(36)$ they force $(d,j,d',j')=(10g,5e,5e,10g)$, with $(g,e)=(1,0)$ for $m=8$, and for $\NL_{10}(36)$ they force $(d,j,d',j')=(10,5,10,70)$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:div} and~\eqref{eq:fibre}, $\psi(T_0)\in\{s,s+9,\dots,s+36\}$ for all $\psi\ne1$, since $0\le|S_\psi|\le16$ and $4\mid|S_\psi|$. The sums of $\psi\ne1$ over one class $\beta^i\langle\beta^8\rangle$ (Gauss periods of index $8$) take the values $-5,-2,1,4$, with multiplicities $10,30,20,20$. For $\LS_8(36)$ and $\NL_{10}(36)$, $T_0$ is a single class and $s\equiv1\pmod 9$, so the values $-5,-2,4$ are excluded; hence these two cases do not occur. For the other values of $m$, a finite check of all $\binom8g$ unions of $g$ classes shows that exactly $4$, $8$, $6$ and $8$ of them, for $g=2,3,4,5$, satisfy $\psi(T_0)\equiv s\pmod 9$ for all $\psi\ne1$. Each of these is a PDS with character values $g$ and $g-9$, which proves~(a). Partial difference sets that are unions of cyclotomic classes have been studied extensively; see~\cite{LMX2025} and the references there.

(b) follows from $d'=5e$. The set $\mu_5$ is the set of nonzero zeros of the elliptic quadratic form $x\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}_{\F_4/\F_2}(x^5)$ on $\F_{16}$, an ovoid of $\mathrm{PG}(3,2)$, and $\Cay(\F_{16},\mu_5)$ is the Clebsch graph. The union of two cosets is the complement of one coset in $\F_{16}^*$.

For~(c), identify the characters of $\F_{16}$ with $\F_{16}$ by $\varphi_c(a)=(-1)^{\mathrm{Tr}(ca)}$. Then $S_1$ is the union of $e$ cosets $c\mu_5$. Each coset is again an ovoid, and a plane $\ker\varphi_a$ ($a\ne0$) meets it in $1$ or $3$ points, so $\sum_{x\in c\mu_5}\varphi_x(a)\in\{1,-3\}$. Hence $\sum_{\varphi\in S_1}\varphi(a)$ is $0$, lies in $\{1,-3\}$, lies in $\{-2,2\}$, or equals $-1$, for $e=0,1,2,3$. Lemma~\ref{lem:trace} then gives $|T_a|\in\{27,36\}$ for $m\ge13$ and $|T_a|\in\{9,18\}$ for $m\le7$. Finally, $|T_a|$ is odd exactly when $0\in T_a$, that is, when $a\in U_0$.
\end{proof}

As a consequence, for $m\ge13$ every $S_\psi$ ($\psi\ne1$) has $4$ or $8$ elements, and for $m\le7$ it has $0$ or $4$. Indeed, $\psi(T_0)\in\{g-9,g\}$ by~(a), and~\eqref{eq:fibre} gives $|S_\psi|=\frac49(\psi(T_0)-s)$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:familyexact}
Of the seven values of $m$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:family}, exactly the six values $m=5,6,13,14,15,16$ occur. Examples are:
\begin{itemize}
\item $m=16$: the graphs I.1--I.6 and I.11, up to isomorphism;
\item $m=15$: IV.1 and IV.2;
\item $m=14$: V.1 and V.2;
\item $m=13$: VI.1;
\item $m=5,6$: $\rho$-invariant PDSs whose connection sets are in the data repository.
\end{itemize}
There is no $\rho$-invariant PDS with parameters $\LS_7(36)$. The parameter sets $\LS_m(36)$ with $m\le7$ were known before, from orthogonal arrays.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The graphs IV--VI and their duals are $\rho$-invariant by construction. For I.1--I.6 and I.11 see the discussion before Proposition~\ref{prop:family}. For $m\le7$, Proposition~\ref{prop:family} fixes $T_0$ and $U_0$ up to multiplication by $\F_{16}^*\times\F_{81}^*$, which commutes with $\rho$, and fixes all ring sizes. Multiplication by $\beta$ permutes the $\NL_g(9)$ unions of classes transitively for $g=2,3$, so for $m=6,7$ the choice of $T_0$ is no restriction; for $m=5$ we fixed one of the two orbits.

With these data fixed, the seeds $T_{\alpha^u}$ are of two kinds. Those of size $9$ are $\{0\}\cup\pm L$ for a line $L$ of $\mathrm{PG}(3,3)$, since a $9$-divisible set of $4$ points meets every plane in $1$ or $4$ points. Those of size $18$ are $\pm P$ for one of the $9{,}880$ sets $P$ of $9$ points all of whose plane sections have size divisible by $3$. An exhaustive join over these catalogs, without a solver, finds solutions for $m=5$ and $m=6$. For $m=7$ it finds none among all $130\cdot 9880$ choices of the first two seeds. The join matches exactly, by hashing, the character sums of the rows with $|S_\psi|=0$, and then checks all rows. Two CP-SAT runs in the model $\langle\rho\rangle$ with these data fixed, using two different normalizations of $(T_0,U_0)$, also prove infeasibility for $m=7$. All connection sets were verified as in Section~\ref{sec:verification}.
\end{proof}

All fourteen graphs I have $U_0=\emptyset$, ring sizes $36$ and zero ring $\Cay(\F_{81},T_0)$ isomorphic to the Brouwer--Haemers graph, although only I.1--I.6 and I.11 are $\rho$-invariant. With $T_0$, $U_0$ and the ring sizes fixed as in Proposition~\ref{prop:family}, CP-SAT solves $m=5,6$ in under a second and proves $m=7$ infeasible in $125$ to $850$ seconds, where the unrestricted model gave no decision in $900$ seconds; for $m=13,14,15$ fixing these data does not help.

\subsection{The order-three graphs}\label{sec:outside}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:family}, $\LS_{12}(36)$ and $\LS_{11}(36)$ need a symmetry other than $\rho$. The forced data are invariant under a fixed-point-free element of order $3$ of $\mathrm{GL}(\F_{16})$, acting by $\F_4$-scalars: the complement of a plane is a union of three $\F_4$-lines, and the grid of three. Combined with the unipotent map $M_3$ on $\F_{81}$ this gives the model $\langle\sigma\rangle$ of Constructions~\ref{con:VII} and~\ref{con:VIII}, with five seeds. In both cases the $\sigma$-invariant candidates for $T_0$ form a single orbit of $108$ sets under the centralizer of $M_3$ in $\mathrm{GL}_4(\F_3)$ (of order $3888$), and all ring sets are of the types $L_1$--$L_4$. The size-$27$ catalog excludes the $40$ sets with a character value $27$: for $a\ne0$, \eqref{eq:fibre} gives $\psi(T_a)\le\frac94\cdot8=18$. A larger group, $\mathrm{diag}(\F_4^*,\F_4^*)$ with a commuting action on $\F_{81}$, gave no PDS in the $89$ (for $\LS_{12}$) and $86$ (for $\LS_{11}$) conjugacy classes we searched completely.

\section{Spreads and completions}\label{sec:spreads}\label{sec:properties}

\subsection{Spreads and cocliques}
Following Haemers and Tonchev~\cite{HaemersTonchev1996}, a \emph{spread} of a strongly regular graph is a partition of its vertex set into Delsarte cliques, of size $1-k/s$, and two spreads are \emph{orthogonal} if every clique of one meets every clique of the other in exactly one vertex. For an $\LS_m(n)$ graph the Delsarte bound is $n$, and so is the Hoffman bound on cocliques. Kova\v{l}~\cite[Lemma~4.1]{KovalMultiOrbit}, building on~\cite[Proposition~4.1]{HaemersTonchev1996}, showed that deleting $j<m$ pairwise orthogonal spreads from an $\LS_m(n)$ graph leaves an $\LS_{m-j}(n)$ graph. Conversely, cliques can be added along a partition into cocliques, and every spanning completion arises this way.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:completion}
Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of an $\LS_m(n)$ graph and $B$ that of an $\LS_{m+1}(n)$ graph on the same vertex set with $A\le B$ entrywise. Then $B-A$ is the adjacency matrix of a disjoint union of $n$ cliques $K_n$, whose vertex sets are cocliques of size $n$ of $A$. Conversely, for every partition of the vertex set into cocliques of size $n$ of $A$, adding all edges inside the parts gives an $\LS_{m+1}(n)$ graph.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $P$ and $Q$ be the orthogonal projectors onto the eigenspaces of $A$ and $B$ for the eigenvalues $n-m$ and $n-m-1$. Then $A=nP-mI+\tfrac mnJ$ and $B=nQ-(m+1)I+\tfrac{m+1}{n}J$, with ranks $m(n-1)$ and $(m+1)(n-1)$, and $PJ=QJ=0$. Put $D=B-A$, a symmetric $0/1$ matrix with zero diagonal that is $(n-1)$-regular. Then $D+I=n(Q-P)+\tfrac1nJ$, and comparing $\operatorname{tr}\bigl((D+I)^2\bigr)=n^2\cdot n$ with this expression gives $\operatorname{tr}\bigl((Q-P)^2\bigr)=n-1=\operatorname{rank}Q-\operatorname{rank}P$. Hence $\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=\operatorname{rank}P$, so $\operatorname{im}P\subseteq\operatorname{im}Q$ and $Q-P$ is a projector. It follows that $(D+I)^2=n(D+I)$, so adjacent vertices of $D$ have the same closed neighborhood, and $D$ is a disjoint union of cliques of order $n$. Their vertex sets are cocliques of $A$ because $A\le B$. Conversely, an $n$-coclique of an $\LS_m(n)$ graph meets the Hoffman bound, so every outside vertex has exactly $m$ neighbors in it. If $T$ is the $0/1$ matrix of the partition (including the diagonal), then $AT=TA=m(J-T)$ and $T^2=nT$, and a direct computation shows that $A+T-I$ satisfies the equation of an $\LS_{m+1}(n)$ graph.
\end{proof}

In a Cayley graph $\Cay(G,D)$, a subgroup $U$ of order $36$ with $U\cap D=\emptyset$, which we call \emph{free} for $D$, has cocliques as cosets. Adding them is the uniform case of Lemma~\ref{lem:completion}; here the character values are also explicit. Dually, if $U^*=U\setminus\{0\}\subseteq D$ then the cosets of $U$ form a spread, and the spreads of two such subgroups are orthogonal exactly when they meet in $\{0\}$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:free}
Let $D$ be a regular PDS in $G$ with parameters $\LS_m(36)$, $m\le34$, and let $U$ be a subgroup of order $36$ with $U\cap D=\emptyset$. Put $U^\perp=\{g:\chi_g(U)=36\}$, a subgroup of order $36$. Then $\chi_g(D)=-m$ for all $g\in U^\perp\setminus\{0\}$, and $D\cup U^*$ is a regular PDS with parameters $\LS_{m+1}(36)$. Consequently, if $U_1,\dots,U_j$ are such subgroups with pairwise intersection $\{0\}$ and $m+j\le35$, then $D\cup U_1^*\cup\dots\cup U_j^*$ is a regular PDS with parameters $\LS_{m+j}(36)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By orthogonality, $\sum_{g\in U^\perp}\chi_g(D)=36\,|D\cap U|=0$, so the $35$ values $\chi_g(D)$, $g\in U^\perp\setminus\{0\}$, add up to $-k=-35m$. Each of them is $36-m$ or $-m$, so all equal $-m$. Since $\chi_g(U^*)=35$ for $g\in U^\perp\setminus\{0\}$ and $-1$ otherwise, $\chi_g(D\cup U^*)$ lies in $\{35-m,\,-m-1\}$ for all $g\ne0$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:char} applies. For the last statement note that $U_2\cap(D\cup U_1^*)=\emptyset$ when $U_1\cap U_2=\{0\}$, and use induction.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:dualspread}
Let $D$ be a regular PDS with parameters $\LS_m(36)$ in $G$, and let $U$ be a subgroup of order $36$ with $U\cap D=\emptyset$, so that $E=D\cup U^*$ has parameters $\LS_{m+1}(36)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:free}). Then $U^\perp\cap D^*=\emptyset$, and $E^*=D^*\cup(U^\perp)^*$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $g\ne0$ we have $\chi_g(U^*)=35$ if $g\in U^\perp$ and $-1$ otherwise. The eigenvalues of $E$ are $r-1$ and $s-1$, where $r-s=36$. For $g\notin U^\perp$, $\chi_g(E)=\chi_g(D)-1$, which equals $r-1$ exactly when $\chi_g(D)=r$. For $g\in U^\perp\setminus\{0\}$, $\chi_g(E)=\chi_g(D)+35\in\{r-1,s-1\}$ forces $\chi_g(D)=s$, so $g\notin D^*$ and $\chi_g(E)=s+35=r-1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Free subgroups}

Among our $35$ graphs, free subgroups exist only for I.6, I.10, III.1--III.4, VIII.1 and VIII.2, whose connection sets $D$ are of type $\LS_m(36)$ with $m=16$, $17$ and $11$. For these graphs, if $U_1,\dots,U_j$ are free subgroups with pairwise intersection $\{0\}$, then $D\cup U_1^*\cup\dots\cup U_j^*$ is an $\LS_{m+j}(36)$ PDS. The free subgroups are as follows (Figure~\ref{fig:spreads}(b)).
\begin{enumerate}
\item I.6 has $5$ free subgroups, and they intersect pairwise in $\{0\}$. So $D_{\mathrm{I.6}}\cup U_1^*\cup\dots\cup U_5^*$ is an $\LS_{21}(36)$ PDS, and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{I.6}}$ arises from an $\LS_{21}(36)$ Cayley graph by deleting five pairwise orthogonal spreads. By the theorem of Bailey and Jungnickel (Section~\ref{sec:known}), five is the largest possible number of subgroups of order $36$ of $G$ that intersect pairwise in $\{0\}$.
\item I.10 has $8$ free subgroups, with $18$ pairs, $8$ triples and one set of four that intersect pairwise in $\{0\}$. So $\Gamma_{\mathrm{I.10}}$ arises from an $\LS_{20}(36)$ Cayley graph by deleting four pairwise orthogonal spreads.
\item III.1 and III.3 have $4$ free subgroups each, and they intersect pairwise in $\{0\}$. III.2 and III.4 have $8$ each, with $12$ pairs, $4$ triples and one set of four that intersect pairwise in $\{0\}$. So every graph III arises from an $\LS_{21}(36)$ Cayley graph by deleting four pairwise orthogonal spreads.
\item VIII.1 and VIII.2 have one free subgroup each, and adding it gives the graphs VII.2 and VII.3 with parameters $\LS_{12}(36)$. The graphs VII.2 and VII.3 are the only ones among ours whose connection set contains $U^*$ for a subgroup $U$ of order $36$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular $\Gamma_{\mathrm{I.6}}$ and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{I.10}}$ arise from an $\LS_{19}(36)$ Cayley graph by deleting three pairwise orthogonal spreads, in $10$ and $8$ ways, and every graph III arises from an $\LS_{19}(36)$ Cayley graph by deleting two.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By exhaustive computation, for all thirty-five connection sets, over the $35\cdot130$ subgroups of order $36$, which are the products of a subgroup of order $4$ of $\F_{16}$ (there are $35$) and a subgroup of order $9$ of $\F_{81}$ (there are $130$). \end{proof}

\subsection{Bush-type Hadamard matrices}
Kova\v{l} constructed $\LS_{n/2-1}(n)$ graphs by deleting two orthogonal spreads from $\LS_{n/2+1}(n)$ graphs coming from Bush-type Hadamard matrices, and asked whether a third orthogonal spread can be deleted, which for $n=36$ would give the parameters $\LS_{16}(36)$~\cite[Section~4.10]{KovalMultiOrbit}. His graphs $\Gamma_0$~\cite[Theorem~4.3]{KovalMultiOrbit} come from symmetric Hadamard matrices $K$ of order $n^2$ of Bush type with unit-diagonal blocks: rows and columns are indexed by $[n]\times[n]$, the diagonal blocks are $J_n$, the other blocks satisfy $K_{ac}J_n=J_nK_{ac}=0$, and $K_{(a,b),(c,b)}=1$. The block rows and block columns are two orthogonal spreads, and deleting both gives an $\LS_{n/2-1}(n)$ graph $\Gamma_K$. Our Cayley graphs answer his question positively for $n=36$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bush}
Let $E$ be an $\LS_{19}(36)$ PDS in $G$, and let $U,V$ be subgroups of order $36$ with $U\cap V=\{0\}$ and $U^*\cup V^*\subseteq E$. Index the vertices $g\in G$ by the pair $(g+U,g+V)$ of cosets, and let $K=2(A+I)-J$, where $A$ is the adjacency matrix of $\Cay(G,E)$. Then $K$ is a symmetric Hadamard matrix of Bush type whose blocks have unit diagonal, $\Cay(G,E)$ is its graph $\Gamma_0$, and $\Cay(G,E\setminus(U\cup V))$ is its graph $\Gamma_K$. If moreover $W$ is a subgroup of order $36$ with $W^*\subseteq E$ and $W\cap U=W\cap V=\{0\}$, then the cosets of $W$ form a third spread of $\Gamma_0$, orthogonal to the block rows and the block columns.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $G=U\oplus V$, each $U$-coset meets each $V$-coset in exactly one vertex, so the indexing is a bijection. $\Cay(G,E)$ is an $\srg(1296,665,340,342)$ with eigenvalues $665$, $17$ and $-19$, so $K$ is a symmetric $\pm1$ matrix with eigenvalues $36$ and $\pm36$, and $K^2=1296I$. The diagonal blocks are $J$ because the $U$-cosets are cliques, and $K_{(a,b),(c,b)}=1$ because the $V$-cosets are cliques. A vertex outside a Delsarte clique of size $36$ has exactly $\mu/(-s)=342/19=18$ neighbors in it~\cite{BvM2022}, so every row of an off-diagonal block has $18$ entries $1$ and $18$ entries $-1$; hence $K_{ac}J=0$, and $JK_{ac}=0$ by symmetry. The adjacency matrix of $\Cay(G,E)$ is $\frac12(J+K)-I$, which is $\Gamma_0$, and deleting the spreads of $U$ and $V$ gives $\Gamma_K$. Finally, $W\cap U=W\cap V=\{0\}$ means that every $W$-coset meets every block row and every block column in one vertex, and $W^*\subseteq E$ makes the $W$-cosets cliques.
\end{proof}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:spreads}, Proposition~\ref{prop:bush} applies to every graph III, with $E=D\cup U^*\cup V^*$ for a pair of orthogonal free subgroups, so the graphs III are graphs $\Gamma_K$ of Bush-type Hadamard matrices developed over $G$, like Kova\v{l}'s $\LS_{17}(36)$ graphs. For I.6 and I.10, with $E=D\cup U^*\cup V^*\cup W^*$, it also gives the third spread. All Bush-type and spread statements above were also checked directly on the matrices.

\subsection{Completions of the graphs I and the parameters \texorpdfstring{$\LS_{17}(36)$}{LS17(36)}}\label{sec:completions}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:completion}, the $\LS_{17}(36)$ graphs that contain one of I.1--I.14 as a spanning subgraph correspond to the partitions of that graph into $36$-cocliques. For I.10, the $36$-cocliques are the $288$ cosets of eight subgroups of order $36$. The exact cover problem on these cosets has $1{,}124$ solutions, which use one, two or three subgroups ($8$, $524$ and $592$ solutions). $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathrm{I}.10)$, of order $20736$, has $24$ orbits on them, and the $24$ graphs are pairwise non-isomorphic. For I.6, the five $36$-cocliques through $0$ are subgroups that pairwise meet only in $0$, so only uniform partitions occur, and they give one graph. The other twelve graphs have no $36$-coclique. Hence exactly $25$ isomorphism classes of $\srg(1296,595,274,272)$ contain one of I.1--I.14 as a spanning subgraph.

Three of the $25$ come from uniform partitions, so they are Cayley graphs $\Cay(G,D\cup U^*)$ of $G$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:free}). One more is vertex-transitive with automorphism group of order $5184$, and its regular subgroups are SmallGroup$(1296,2797)$ and SmallGroup$(1296,2848)$, both non-abelian. So its connection sets are non-abelian partial difference sets, and it is not a Cayley graph of an abelian group. Another has a transitive automorphism group of order $2592$ but no regular subgroup, so it is vertex-transitive but not a Cayley graph. The remaining $20$ are not vertex-transitive. None of the $25$ is the graph of an orthogonal array: each has an edge whose $274$ common neighbors can be colored with $33$ colors, so it lies in no $36$-clique. All $25$ are pairwise non-isomorphic, and none is isomorphic to III.1--III.4, to the two graphs of Remark~\ref{rem:koval595}, or to Kova\v{l}'s multi-orbit graphs~\cite{KovalMultiOrbit}. The graphs, the exact covers, and independent verifiers are in the data repository.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:koval595}
Two further Cayley graphs of $\F_{16}\times\F_{81}$ with the parameters $\LS_{17}(36)$ were found by A.~Kova\v{l} on 20 October 2026 by a CP-SAT search over unions of orbits of a group of semilinear maps of $\F_{16}\times\F_{81}$ (with AI assistance); see also~\cite{BrouwerTable} and the list of tried methods at \url{https://tonykoval.github.io/srg-database/library.html}. The search ran in the same two models as III.1--III.4: invariance under $\mu_4$ on the second coordinate, together with the Galois map $(a,b)\mapsto(a^2,b^3)$ for one graph and $(a,b)\mapsto(a^2,b^{27})$ for the other. Both have automorphism groups of order $20736$. With III.1--III.4 they form six pairwise non-isomorphic graphs (nauty and bliss canonical forms), so these two models contain at least six isomorphism classes. Both graphs, which he provided, are in the data repository.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:595}]
The nine Cayley graphs of $G$ are III.1--III.4, the two graphs of Remark~\ref{rem:koval595} and the three uniform completions; the other two vertex-transitive graphs and the count of $25$ are the completions above. All thirty-one graphs, together with Kova\v{l}'s multi-orbit graphs, are pairwise non-isomorphic by nauty and bliss canonical forms and by their four-clique counts.
\end{proof}

\section{Relation to known constructions}\label{sec:known}
None of the constructions of Latin square and negative Latin square type PDSs that we checked gives any of our parameter sets in an abelian group of order $1296=2^4\cdot3^4$. Partial congruence partitions give $\LS_m(36)$ only for $m\le5$, since at most $\min(2^2+1,3^2+1)=5$ subgroups of order $36$ can meet pairwise trivially (Bailey and Jungnickel~\cite[Theorem~2.4]{Ma1994}). The product theorems for packings~\cite[Theorems 3.15, 6.8]{JedwabLi2021}, which include Polhill's construction~\cite{Polhill2008}, combine packings with a common $t>1$; every LP-packing listed in~\cite[Table~1.1]{JedwabLi2021} lies in a $p$-group with $t$ a power of $p$, and every NLP-packing in~\cite[Table~1.2]{JedwabLi2021} with $t>2$ in a $2$- or $3$-group, so in this group the only combination gives $\NL_{17}(36)$ and $\NL_{18}(36)$. Momihara's families~\cite[Theorems 1.4, 1.5]{Momihara2021} need $2m\mid q_i+1$ for all $i$, which is impossible for $q_i=16,81$. The cyclotomy of Fern\'andez-Alcober, Kwashira and Mart\'\i nez~\cite{FKM2010} on $\F_{16}\times\F_{81}$, which by~\cite[Section~1]{Ott2016b} also covers Ott's generalizations~\cite{Ott2016b,Ott2016a}, has order $e\mid5$; its PDSs are unions of orbits of a subgroup of $\F_{16}^*\times\F_{81}^*$ of order $1200/e\ge240$, while our connection sets have multiplicative stabilizers of order at most $20$ (Table~\ref{tab:classes}), and an exhaustive check shows that no union of such orbits has any of our parameter sets.

\section{Open questions}\label{sec:open}
\emph{Partial difference sets with $7\le m\le10$.} Our graphs, the partial congruence partitions, the $\rho$-invariant sets of Theorem~\ref{thm:familyexact} and the added spreads give $\LS_m(36)$ PDSs in $\Z_2^4\times\Z_3^4$ for $m\le6$ and $11\le m\le26$, and their complements. For $7\le m\le10$ we know none. Theorem~\ref{thm:fibredata} fixes the binary data, Theorem~\ref{thm:familyexact} excludes the model $\langle\rho\rangle$, and for $m=8,9,10$ CP-SAT gave no decision in $30$ minutes in the model $\langle\sigma\rangle$ with these data fixed; the ring join has not been tried there.

\emph{Orthogonal arrays.} None of our graphs comes from an orthogonal array. The existence of $\mathrm{OA}(36,m)$ is open for $m\ge11$.

\emph{Negative Latin square parameters.} The parameter sets $\NL_m(36)$ with $5\le m\le15$, and their complements, are open. A version of Theorem~\ref{thm:fibredata} for negative Latin square parameters would be a natural next step.

\emph{Completions and switching.} Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} reduces the $\LS_{17}(36)$ graphs containing a given $\LS_{16}(36)$ graph to an exact cover problem; which other graphs with these parameters admit such partitions? Which of the $2^{80}$ switched graphs of Section~\ref{sec:switching} are isomorphic?

\emph{Explaining the alphabet.} Lemma~\ref{lem:alphabet} explains why few local types are possible, but not why the same ones recur across different $m$. A hand proof of the existence of any of the graphs IV--VIII would also be of interest.

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verification}
All checks pass.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A standalone program (plain Python, no code shared with the search) builds $\Cay(G,D)$ with its own field arithmetic and checks $D=-D$, $0\notin D$, the degree and the common neighbors of all $839{,}160$ vertex pairs, for Constructions~\ref{con:I}--\ref{con:VIII} as printed, all thirty-five connection sets and the $\rho$-invariant sets with $m=5,6$. The graphs IV.1--VIII.1 were also verified from their original connection sets in $(\Z/6\Z)^4$ by the identity $A^2=(k-\mu)I+(\lambda-\mu)A+\mu J$.
\item Kova\v{l}'s independent verifier \texttt{verify\_srg.py}~\cite{KovalNew} was run on all graphs of this paper, including the switched graphs and the completions.
\item Isomorphism classes and automorphism groups were computed with nauty and, independently, with bliss.
\item The switched graphs and the completions have their own standard-library verifiers, which check all vertex pairs from the graph6 files. The completions are rebuilt byte for byte from two seeds and the partitions, and their non-abelian connection sets are checked in GAP by counting all differences.
\item The finite checks in the proofs (Theorem~\ref{thm:fibredata}, Proposition~\ref{prop:family}, Theorem~\ref{thm:familyexact}, Proposition~\ref{prop:spreads}) and the structural claims are done by separate programs. All are in the data repository.
\end{enumerate}
